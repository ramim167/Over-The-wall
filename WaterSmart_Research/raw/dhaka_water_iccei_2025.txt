Abstract 
In urban areas like Dhaka, point-of-use water quality monitoring is often overlooked due to the high cost and 
difficulty of widespread sampling. This study evaluates the spatial variation of key water quality parameters 
hardness, sulfate, chloride, and nitrate ions in Dhaka Water Supply and Sewerage Authority (DWASA) supplied 
tap water across 13 locations in Dhaka city. Water samples were collected directly from household taps to 
represent actual consumer exposure. The analysis revealed notable variations among locations. Amin Bazar 
recorded the highest water hardness (300 mg/l), while Kuril showed the lowest (33 mg/l). Chloride levels were 
highest in Amin Bazar (233.93 mg/l) and lowest in Rampura (39.99 mg/l). Sulfate ion concentrations were 
mostly below 20 mg/l, except in Amin Bazar (90 mg/l) and Kamlapur (42 mg/l), indicating local anomalies. 
Nitrate was detected only in Tongi (15 mg/l) and Amin Bazar (20 mg/l), with all other locations showing 0 mg/l. 
These variations suggest the influence of pipe age, source water quality, and potential infrastructure issues. The 
study emphasizes the need for regular and localized monitoring to help identify areas with deteriorating pipelines 
and support Water Supply Authorities (WSA) in prioritizing maintenance efforts effectively. 
 
Keywords: Dhaka Water Supply and Sewerage Authority (DWASA), Water Supply Authorities (WSA) 
 
1 Introduction 
 
 Access to clean and safe drinking water is essential for human health, well-being, and sustainable urban 
development (E. Cicia et al., 2010; Crase et al., 2005). In densely populated cities like Dhaka, the capital of 
Bangladesh, the provision of safe and reliable tap water has become increasingly challenging due to rapid 
urbanization, industrial expansion, population growth, and aging infrastructure (Cidu et al., 2010). With over 
10.28 million residents Dhaka faces mounting pressure on its municipal water supply system, resulting in 
widespread concerns over water safety and quality. (Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics [BBS], 2022). Water 
contamination in urban settings can stem from several factors, including microbial pathogens, heavy metals, and 
chemical pollutants, often aggravated by poor water treatment practices, outdated pipelines, and illegal industrial 
discharges (Allaire et al., 2018; Trueman et al., 2019). Numerous studies have shown that insufficient oversight 
and lack of maintenance in water distribution systems can lead to significant deterioration in tap water quality, 
heightening the risk of gastrointestinal illnesses, heavy metal toxicity, and other waterborne diseases (Cidu et al., 
2010; Kachel et al., 2007; Zivin et al., 2011). Furthermore, public perception plays a critical role in water use 
behavior. Consumers often assess water quality based not only on objective safety parameters but also on 
sensory attributes like taste, odor, and color (Um et al., 2002; McDonald and Rundle, 2006). In Dhaka, this dis-
connect between perceived and actual water quality has led to a growing dependence on bottled water and home 
filtration systems, reflecting broader issues of trust in public water utilities and regulatory enforcement (Crase, 
2007; Zivin et al., 2011). Globally, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) emphasize the 
importance of achieving universal and equitable access to safe and affordable drinking water by 2030 (UN, 
2015). However, in many low- and middle-income urban centers like Dhaka, progress remains constrained by 
infrastructural, financial, and governance limitations. This study aims to evaluate both the objective and 
perceived quality of tap water in selected areas of Dhaka city. It compares municipal and bottled water sources in 
light of national and international drinking water standards, while also examining public perception through 
household-level surveys. The findings are intended to inform policy strategies for improving water quality 
governance, enhancing infrastructure, and protecting public health in rapidly urbanizing environment.

2.2 Determination of Water Quality Parameters 
 
2.2.1 Chloride Ion (Cl-) 
 
2.2.1.1 Principle of the Test (Mohr’s Method) 
After collecting the water samples, they were transported to the laboratory and analyzed within 24 hours to 
ensure accuracy in water quality assessment. For the determination of chloride ions, a titration method using a 
standard silver nitrate (AgNO₃) solution was employed. The 0.0141 N AgNO₃ solution was prepared by 
accurately dissolving 2.395 g of silver nitrate in distilled water, transferring it to a 1-liter volumetric flask, and 
diluting it to the mark, as depicted in Fig. 3a. Since AgNO₃ is photosensitive, it was stored in a dark bottle. A 5% 
potassium chromate (K₂CrO₄) solution was used as an indicator, prepared by dissolving 5g of K₂CrO₄ in 100 mL 
of distilled water, then filtered and stored in a sealed container as depicted in Fig. 3b. A standard sodium chloride 
(NaCl) solution of 0.0141 N was also prepared by dissolving 0.824 g of pre-dried NaCl (at 105°C) in distilled 
water and diluting it to 1 liter as depicted in Fig. 3c. For the titration, 25 mL of the water sample was pipetted 
into a clean conical flask, followed by the addition of 1 mL of potassium chromate indicator. The sample was 
titrated with the silver nitrate solution until the appearance of a permanent red-brown color indicated the 
endpoint, as depicted in Fig. 3d, resulting from the formation of silver chromate (Ag₂CrO₄) after all chloride ions 
had reacted with silver to form silver chloride (AgCl). The volume of AgNO₃ consumed was recorded for 
chloride concentration calculation. The concentration of chloride ions (in mg/L) was calculated using the 
following formula: 
 
Chloride (mg/L) = (V×N×35.45×1000)/Sample Volume                                                                                       (1) 
 
 
 
Where, V = volume of AgNO₃ used (mL), N = normality of AgNO₃, and 35.45 = molar mass of Cl⁻  
  
2.2.2 Hardness Determination 
For total hardness determination, a clean 5 mL plastic beaker was rinsed and filled with the water sample. Five 
drops of hardness buffer and one drop of Calmagite indicator were added through the cap port, and the solution 
was gently swirled. A red-violet color indicated the presence of hardness ions. Using a titration syringe, EDTA 
titrant (HI 3812-0) was added dropwise with swirling after each drop. The color shifted from red-violet to purple, 
and finally to blue as shown in Fig. 4, marking the endpoint. The volume of EDTA used was recorded and 
multiplied by 300 to express total hardness in mg/L as CaCO₃. 
 
2.2.3 Sulfate Ion Determination 
Sulfate concentration in the water sample was determined using a visual turbidity-based method. A plastic vessel 
was filled with 50 mL of the sample, followed by the addition of one packet of HI 38000A-0 Sulfate Reagent, 
and the solution was gently swirled to ensure complete dissolution. Two drops of Complexing Agent and two 
spoons of HI 38000B-0 Reagent were then added with thorough mixing. The mixture was allowed to stand 
undisturbed for 5 minutes to allow for full reaction development. The test tube was placed on a white surface, 
and the sample was added dropwise using a plastic pipette until the black spot at the bottom was no longer 
visible as depicted in Fig. 5A and Fig. 5B. The sulfate concentration was determined by reading the liquid level 
in the tube, expressed in mg/L (ppm). 
 
2.2.4 Nitrate Ion Determination 
Nitrate concentration in the water sample was determined using a colorimetric method. A plastic vessel was 
rinsed and filled with 10 mL of the sample, after which one packet of HI 3834-0 reagent was added. The vessel 
was capped and shaken until the reagent fully dissolved. The resulting solution was transferred into a color 
comparator cube and left undisturbed for 4 minutes to allow complete color development. The final color was 
then visually compared with a standard reference scale as shown in Fig. 6, and the corresponding concentration 
was recorded in mg/L (ppm). A noticeable color change in the sample collected from Amin Bazar confirmed the 
presence of nitrate in the water. This colorimetric response confirms that nitrate contamination is present at

3.1 Sulfate 
The measured sulfate concentrations in all the selected areas of Dhaka city ranged from <20 mg/L to a maximum 
of 90 mg/L in Amin Bazar as shown in Table 2, which is well below both the Bangladesh and WHO guideline 
value of 400 mg/L as shown in Table 3. According to standard classification, sulfate levels below 250 mg/L are 
considered safe for drinking and general domestic use. Most locations such as Jasim Uddin, Dhanmondi, 
Mohammadpur, Rampura, and others showed sulfate concentrations below 20 mg/L, indicating very low sulfate 
presence and suggesting minimal anthropogenic influence or effective protection of groundwater sources. 
Moderately higher values were observed in Mirpur (23 mg/L), Motijheel (27 mg/L), Kamlapur (42 mg/L), and 
Gabtoli (27 mg/L), likely due to moderate urban runoff and sewage contamination. Amin Bazar exhibited the 
highest sulfate level (90 mg/L), probably linked to landfill leachate, poor sanitation, and open dumping. Though 
all observed values fall within safe limits, elevated sulfate concentrations even below the threshold can 
contribute to scale buildup in pipelines, reduce the efficiency of water heaters, and slightly alter taste, affecting

3.2 Hardness 
The measured hardness of water samples from Dhaka city ranged from 33 mg/L at Kuril, classified as slightly 
hard according to WHO guidelines, to 300 mg/L at Amin Bazar, which falls into the very hard category as 
depicted in Table 1. Specifically, Kuril (33 mg/L) was slightly hard, Jasim Uddin (45 mg/L) and Motijheel (51 
mg/L) were also in the slightly hard range, while Abdullahpur (60 mg/L) and Kochu Khet (90 mg/L) were 
classified as moderately hard as shown in Fig. 7. Locations like Tongi (126 mg/L), Mirpur (144 mg/L), Rampura 
(150 mg/L), Dhanmondi (105 mg/L), and Mohammadpur (150 mg/L) were in the hard category, while Gabtoli 
(195 mg/L) and Amin Bazar (300 mg/L) were in the very hard category. According to Bangladesh standards 
(150–500 mg/L) and WHO’s aesthetic limit (200 mg/L), most samples were within acceptable limits, but Amin 
Bazar (300 mg/L) exceeded the WHO guideline, indicating potential problems such as scaling in plumbing

3.3 Chloride Ion (Cl-) 
The chloride ion (Cl⁻) concentrations measured across various locations ranged from 39.99 mg/L to 233.93 mg/L 
as shown in Table 2. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the guideline value for chloride in 
drinking water is 250 mg/L, primarily for aesthetic reasons such as taste and corrosivity. Bangladesh standards 
(BSTI) also recommend a range of 150–600 mg/L as depicted in Table 3. While all tested sites were within the 
permissible limits, Amin Bazar (233.93 mg/L) and Gabtoli (215.93 mg/L) were close to the upper boundary, 
indicating potential future risks. High chloride levels can corrode metal components in pumps, pipelines, and 
plumbing systems, leading to reduced equipment lifespan, leaks, and increased maintenance costs. Corrosion 
may also cause toxic metals like lead or iron to leach into the water supply, posing health risks. Although 
chloride itself is not harmful at these levels, the associated effects on infrastructure and secondary contamination 
are concerning. Areas like Mohammadpur and Kochukhet showed moderately high values and require regular 
monitoring. In contrast, locations such as Rampura, Kuril, and Motijheel exhibited low chloride levels and pose 
minimal risk. Preventive measures like using corrosion-resistant materials, regular testing, and public awareness 
are recommended to ensure long-term water safety and infrastructure integrity. 
 
3.4 Nitrate (NO₃⁻) concentrations 
Nitrate (NO₃⁻) concentrations in the sampled locations spanned 0 mg/L to 20 mg/L, with eleven sites including 
Jasim Uddin, Abdullahpur, Kuril, Gabtoli, Mohammadpur, Mirpur, Kamalapur, Motijheel, Dhanmondi, Rampura 
and Kochukhet showing no detectable nitrate, while Tongi registered about 15 mg/L and Amin Bazar about 20 
mg/L as depicted in Table 2. These values are comfortably below the World Health Organization (WHO) 
guideline of 10 mg/L and the Bangladesh drinking-water standard of 50 mg/L as shown in Table 3, yet the two 
elevated sites warrant attention because nitrate can convert to nitrite in the body and impair oxygen transport, 
triggering methemoglobinemia in infants and posing long-term risks such as thyroid dysfunction. Rising nitrate 
often signals agricultural runoff, leaking sewage or aging plumbing that allows contaminated surface water to 
infiltrate supply lines; if unchecked, it can overload treatment units, foul pumps and hasten biofilm growth in 
distribution pipes. Consequently, Tongi and Amin Bazar should undergo more frequent monitoring, source-
tracking and possibly improvements to wastewater control and pipe integrity, while the other locations remain 
low-risk provided routine surveillance and good agricultural and plumbing practices are maintained. 
 
 
4. Conclusion 
 
The groundwater quality assessment conducted across various locations of Dhaka city indicates that while the 
measured parameters including sulfate, hardness, chloride, and nitrate are within both the Bangladesh National 
Standards and WHO guideline values, spatial variations suggest localized sources of contamination and differing 
hydrogeological conditions. Amin Bazar consistently exhibited the highest concentrations for multiple 
parameters, particularly sulfate (90 mg/L), hardness (300 mg/L), chloride (233.93 mg/L), and nitrate (20 mg/L), 
pointing toward the influence of landfill leachate, poor sanitation infrastructure, and possible surface water 
intrusion. In contrast, residential areas such as Dhanmondi, Mohammadpur, and Jasim Uddin recorded lower 
contaminant levels, indicating relatively protected aquifers and effective groundwater abstraction practices.
